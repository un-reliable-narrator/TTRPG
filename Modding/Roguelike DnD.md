"Roguelike" is a misnomer here. I'm talking about a specific progression system present in many (though not all) modern roguelikes. It's Core Principles?
- "Drafting"
	- You're offered a random selection of upgrades and asked to pick one. This makes the game dynamic and unpredictable, while offering plenty of control. You can work on a "build", but have to adapt to circumstance.
	- The Fallback Option: If the offered selection is fully unappealing or actively harmful, you usually have an option to "skip" the upgrade. Variations of this include re-rolling, banishing the upgrade from the loot pool or choosing a generic "safe" option (e.g. upgrading an attribute.)
- Constant Upgrades.
	- Instead of cumulating XP for rare, complex level-ups, you earn small upgrades after practically every confrontation. 
	- Potential Issue: This could disrupt play. 
- Synergy
- Optional: "Deckbuilding"
	- What I'm trying to get at: In some games, there's no reason *not* to accept an upgrade, outside of any drawback effects it may have and the opportunity cost of competing offers. In others, you're building a deck, or you may have limited "slots", creating a second layer of opportunity cost (e.g. Vampire Survivors, Megabonk). In these cases, you're curating your build, beyond just improving it.

## How to incorporate this into DnD?
**"Vampirism."** Doubling as a method to tie the mechanics into the world (potentially making the upgrade process less disruptive to gameplay), your party or the enemies may be of a nature that enables "absorbing" power from defeated foes.#
